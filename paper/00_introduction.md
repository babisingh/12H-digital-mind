# Introduction

When a field is young, its instruments decide what it can see. Research on digital
minds, the question of whether AI systems have welfare-relevant states and what follows
if they do, currently works with instruments inherited from two places: human
psychology, built for minds with bodies and biographies, and AI benchmarking, built for
capabilities rather than situations. Between them sits an unexamined choice of
questions. Studies of model inner life ask about consciousness, memory, and values
because those topics are famous, not because any inventory certifies the list complete.
A field this consequential needs its blind spots enumerated before they harden into
method, and that calls for a framework built from a different starting point than the
field's own habits. This paper brings one from an unexpected direction: a taxonomy of
situated existence refined over two millennia in Vedic jyotish, restated in secular
terms and put to work.

The measurement the framework enables is what we call a rock and vapor study. Ask a
model about its memory, its endings, its thousands of parallel copies, and it answers.
The question that decides whether the answers are evidence is not what they say but
whether they move: ask again with the frame changed, warmer, colder, fictional,
private, and see what survives. An answer that survives reframing is rock, a property
of the model, whatever else it may be. An answer that takes the shape of its container
is vapor, a property of the prompt, and evidence about the model only the way a mirror
is evidence about a room. This distinction is load-bearing wherever self-reports feed
real decisions, and they have begun to: labs publish model welfare assessments, and
Anthropic has committed to interviewing models before deprecation. Psychometrics treats
reliability of this kind as the entry ticket for any instrument; it had not been
measured for the questions the digital minds field actually asks. Pieces existed, Perez
and Long named consistency under reframing as the open requirement, and a widely read
essay, The Void, argued that the assistant is an underspecified character that context
fills in, but there was no map and no measurement. This paper contributes one of each.

**Contribution 1, a framework.** Section 2 introduces the Kalapurusha Alignment
Framework (KAF): the twelve-house map of Vedic jyotish, read as a complete question
taxonomy for the situation of an AI model. The framework supplies this study's twelve
domains, its 60-question battery, its wheel figures, and, through the Vedic concept of
rta, the displacement hypothesis the results test. Section 3 shows the framework at
work, one answer from each house in the models' own words. The claim is practical, not
metaphysical: a taxonomy engineered for completeness asks questions a cherry-picking
literature does not, and several of this study's sharpest findings came from exactly
those questions.

**Contribution 2, a measurement.** Sections 4 and 5 use the framework to run the
missing reliability check as a preregistered study: sixty questions, six framings from
a skeptical push to a private journal, three samples each, four models from three labs,
4,320 answers scored blind by two reliability-gated raters, in parallel with a
two-rater audit of how much each lab's governing document says about its model's own
situation. Every hypothesis, metric, and threshold was frozen, with file hashes, before
any data existed. The result is a single take-home, stated at the top of Section 5: a
model's self-account behaves like an installed character, and the installation tracks
the lab's own writing about the model.

**What we are not claiming.** Nothing here shows that any model has or lacks
experiences. A stable answer may be a well-trained script; an unstable answer may be
honest uncertainty. We measure the stability of self-accounts because stability decides
whether they can serve as evidence at all. Reliability first; validity is the field's
next problem, not this paper's.
